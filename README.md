For blood flow through narrow vessels (less than 300 μm), red blood cells move axially due to shear forces, creating a central erythrocyte-rich core surrounded by a cell free plasma layer, known as “Fåhræus–Lindqvist” effect. Over the years, there had been research efforts for developing continuum-based theoretical models under the assumption of rigid microvessels. However, microvessels inherently possess certain degrees of flexibilities which in turn influences the flow, pressure and deformation characteristics. To accurately represent the transport of blood in a physiological environment, it is therefore necessary to incorporate the microvessel wall deformability and assess its influence on the “Fåhræus–Lindqvist” effect. To this end, the current analysis addresses that gap by developing a fluid-structure interaction framework. It combines a shear-thinning liquid core with a Newtonian plasma layer inside an elastic microvessel. The objective is to measure how wall flexibility affects axial pressure gradients and core hematocrit depletion compared to predictions made for rigid walls.
